# Thesis and ethical argument

## Thesis

CHORUS treats distorted public meaning as a concurrent social-system problem,
not merely as a collection of false statements. An ambiguous artifact can gain
persuasive force through repetition, aesthetic fluency, familiar linguistic
form, trusted relationships, market incentives, institutional delay, and
person-directed attribution before anyone resolves its factual boundary.

The simulation therefore asks the player to inhabit six seats whose problems
continue at the same time. A choice that is locally understandable can alter
the attention, trust, verification capacity, blame, or common ground available
elsewhere. The game does not ask only whether a claim is true. It asks what was
observed, what an audience inferred, which relationship or status an account
protects, who can reply, and what corrective route remains carryable under
pressure.

## Intended learning movement

The player is not told the analytic answer at the entrance. Early surfaces show
records, uncertainty, presentation, reply conditions, and changing house
signals. Concrete actions are offered in the language of the occupied seat.
Only after all six rooms close does the complete receipt name the represented
patterns.

This sequence supports three movements:

1. **Notice without premature classification.** Distinguish artifact from
   interpretation and keep unknowns open.
2. **Act inside constraints.** Experience how evidence, authority,
   relationships, distribution, fatigue, and concurrent consequences change
   which repairs are possible.
3. **Reconstruct causality.** Compare the final authored interior, action
   history, and cross-room receipts against the player's earlier reading.

The intended outcome is not suspicion toward every warm, terse, political,
regional, professional, or culturally situated exchange. It is a more precise
habit of separating record, inference, incentive, power, and consequence.

## Why one concurrent night

Six independent stories would allow the player to optimize each room without
carrying its externalities. One concurrent night makes three claims playable:

- attention and correction capacity are shared resources;
- a social condition can travel without the same content traveling; and
- a room can be locally complete while its wider consequences continue.

Room switching never advances time by itself. Accepted choices and explicit
clock movement do. The player is free to inspect another seat without being
penalized for reading, pausing, or using assistive technology.

## Why there is no score

A single score would collapse distinct questions: factual trace, distribution,
trust, blame, visible common ground, discernment, and follow-through. It would
also imply that one metric could compensate for a violation of another. CHORUS
instead preserves a causal receipt and separate ledgers.

The absence of a score does not mean consequences are vague. Every accepted
choice has bounded metric effects, a duration, a local receipt, five remote
receipts, and, where applicable, persistent support, linguistic, relational,
pressure, framework, or last-resort records.

## Interpretation boundary

CHORUS is an explanatory fictional model. It is not:

- a prediction of how a real person will act;
- a diagnosis or personality assessment;
- a trust, truthfulness, danger, or compatibility score;
- a guide for inferring motive from tone, age, identity, region, class,
  profession, vocabulary, coalition, or register;
- a reconstruction of a particular real conflict; or
- an operational guide for manipulation, targeting, evasion, or harassment.

The player can see a protagonist's authored interior because the player
temporarily occupies that fictional seat. The simulation does not generalize
that access to real people.

## Governing distinctions

### Motive is not truth

A rumor can protect its initiator and still contain one bounded true detail. A
defensive motive does not prove the rumor false; the event record tests the
claim. Conversely, one true detail does not validate a trait generalization.

### Understanding is not exoneration

A blocked ideal can name what the protagonist is protecting, which emotion is
overtaking follow-through, why that pressure is present, and which systems are
missing. That explanation neither excuses the next harmful choice nor declares
it inevitable.

### Presentation is not interior

Warm presentation can carry instrumental intent. Cool presentation can carry
care, duty, fear, or cognitive load. Presentation temperature is a property of
one represented exchange, not a permanent type.

### Code is not identity or worldview

An actor may learn several registers through family, peer, regional,
socioeconomic, professional, institutional, political, and platform contexts.
The register selected for an audience does not determine belief, competence,
honesty, or worth. Two actors can share a register while holding different
models of what the exchange is for.

### Fatigue is not ignorance

The model separates discernment from enactment. Platform exposure can make a
prosocial action harder to carry without making the protagonist unable to
recognize it. Fatigue explains a capacity condition; it does not transfer
responsibility to the person absorbing the cost.

## Ethical safeguards

- Incidents, people, places, institutions, and platforms are fictional
  composites.
- Person-directed claims remain low-stakes and nonviolent.
- Bounded conduct and character generalization are always separate records.
- A target's silence remains unknown when reply access is absent or unequal.
- Youth roles remain in ordinary peer settings.
- Instrumental coordination remains abstract and non-operational.
- No identity, protected characteristic, accent, warmth, reserve, age, or
  eccentricity acts as evidence of coordination.
- Registers use readable discourse patterns rather than accent imitation,
  caricatured spelling, or unexplained shorthand.
- Ground truth is immutable and excluded from propagation coefficients.
- Every beat retains a non-amplifying route.

## Audience

CHORUS is designed for players willing to read uncertainty, inhabit imperfect
seats, and revisit an early interpretation. Its PG boundary permits use in
household, classroom, library, civic, and professional-learning contexts while
avoiding the assumption that younger or more general audiences need flattened
social systems.

The experience is not designed as a timed assessment. It does not rank the
player, infer traits from play, or claim that one night measures real-world
judgment.
